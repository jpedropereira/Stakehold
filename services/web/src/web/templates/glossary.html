Glossary

What each figure on your holdings, in Labs and on Risk means. Amounts are in euros unless a figure says otherwise.

Units
How many shares of the holding you own. An account's own page calls this Quantity.
Avg cost
What you paid per unit, averaged over your buys and including fees. Each buy is converted to euros at the exchange rate on the day you made it, so the euro average and the trading-currency average aren't linked by any single rate.
Cost basis
What you paid for the units you still hold: units × avg cost. Exchange rates moving after a buy don't change it.
Market value
What the units you hold are worth at the latest closing price. The smaller line beneath is the same value in the currency the holding trades in. It says “Mixed currencies” when your accounts hold the ticker in different currencies.
Weight
The holding's share of the market value of all your priced holdings. A holding still waiting for prices has no weight, and isn't counted as worth zero.
Unrealized return
Market value − cost basis: how much the price has moved since you bought. It leaves out dividends. The percentage is that amount ÷ cost basis.
Dividends received
The dividends paid to you, net of withholding tax where your broker records the tax against the dividend.
Total return
Unrealized return + dividends received: what the holding has made for you so far. The percentage is that amount ÷ cost basis, so it measures what your money made, and depends on when and how much you bought.
Prices as of
The trading day of the latest closing price used. Prices update daily, and returns are recalculated a few times a day.
Backtest
How a mix of assets would have done if you had bought it at the start of each period and held it until the latest day every asset has a price. Labs starts from your current holdings, with today's weights. Returns are in euros, include dividends, and are percentages only: how much you hold plays no part.
Buy and hold
The mix is bought at its weights on the period's first day and never rebalanced, so an asset that rises comes to weigh more: the weights drift. Each period starts again from the same weights on its own first day. The risk figures are labelled “buy & hold (drifting)” for this reason: they measure the mix as it drifted, not one kept at its starting weights.
Max
Each asset's whole history. For the mix, it starts on the first day the oldest asset has a price, and each other asset joins on its own first day, when the mix is split again by the weights you set. A shorter period an asset doesn't cover shows “Not enough history” for that asset, and the mix leaves it out of that period.
Risk windows
Labs measures risk over the last 1, 3, 5 and 10 years, and over Max, all ending on the latest day every asset has a price. Every row, the mix's and each asset's, uses the same window, so the figures can be compared. That window can start no earlier than the youngest asset's first price, and the page names that asset. A window longer than that history shows “Not enough history”. Weekly figures use whole weeks, Friday to Friday, inside the window.
Returns used
How many daily and weekly returns a window's figures rest on. Fewer returns make a figure less certain. Below a minimum (52 weeks for volatility, Sharpe and Sortino; a year for CAGR, drawdown and Calmar) a figure isn't shown, and its cell says why.
CAGR
The window's historical return as a yearly rate: the return per year that, compounded, gives the window's whole return. In euros, dividends included. Measured on daily returns. It isn't annualised for windows under a year.
Volatility
How much the historical weekly returns varied, scaled to a year: their standard deviation × √52. A higher figure means a bumpier ride. It counts rises and falls alike.
Max drawdown
The largest historical fall from a high to a later low within the window, as a percentage of the high. Measured on daily values. It depends on where the window starts: one that starts mid-fall understates it.
Drawdown
On each day, how far the mix was below its highest value so far in the period. 0% is a new high. The chart shows the historical path the max drawdown is the deepest point of.
Sharpe ratio
The historical return above the risk-free rate per unit of volatility, from weekly returns, per year. The risk-free rate is the euro overnight rate: €STR, and before October 2019 EONIA, adjusted to match. The ± is one standard error: the true figure lies inside it only about two times in three, and it is a lower bound, since real returns have fatter tails than the estimate assumes. Over three years it is about ± 0.6, so two Sharpe ratios that close can't be told apart.
Sortino ratio
As the Sharpe ratio, but dividing by the downside deviation: only the weeks the historical return fell short of the risk-free rate count as risk.
Calmar ratio
Historical CAGR ÷ the size of the max drawdown, over the same window: how much a year the mix or portfolio made for each unit of its worst fall. Under three years it is marked “under 3 years”, since one bad stretch dominates a short window.

Ex-post
Measured on what you actually held: the daily time-weighted return of your invested holdings, in euros with dividends included. Buying and selling change the next day's weights, not the return. Cash isn't tracked yet, so it plays no part. These are historical figures for each window, which ends on the latest day every holding has a price.
Cumulative return
How much the portfolio grew or shrank over the whole window, compounding each day's return.
Downside deviation
Like volatility, but counting only the weeks that did worse than the risk-free rate (the euro overnight rate, €STR). It measures the bumps that hurt.
1-day VaR
Value at risk: a one-day loss the portfolio's daily returns exceeded on only 5% of days (95%) or 1% of days (99%) in the window. It is a historical threshold, not a limit: worse days happened.
1-day CVaR
Conditional value at risk: the average loss on the days beyond the VaR, the worst 5% or 1% of days. It says how bad those days were, where VaR only says where they begin.
{# Against a benchmark (RK-14), on /labs and /risk. Historical, like the rest (R15). #}
Benchmark
The index fund the figures under “Against” compare with: VWCE.DE, a world index fund in euros, unless you pick another. The same one is used for every window. A window that starts before its history is greyed, never measured against a different index, since two indices answer different questions: pick one with a longer history, such as IWDA.AS, to compare over ten years.
Beta
How much the historical weekly return moved for each 1% move in the benchmark's. 1 moved with it, above 1 moved more, below 1 moved less. Measured on weekly returns in euros, so it needs two years of overlap with the benchmark.
Correlation
How closely the historical weekly returns rose and fell together with the benchmark's, from −1 (always opposite) to 1 (always together). It says nothing about how far each moved; beta does.
Tracking error
How far the historical weekly returns strayed from the benchmark's, scaled to a year: the standard deviation of the weekly differences × √52. Near 0 means it moved like the benchmark.
Information ratio
The historical return above the benchmark's, per year, divided by the tracking error, from weekly returns: how much was gained or lost relative to the benchmark for each unit of straying from it.
{# Ex-ante (RK-18), for /risk's second section. Historical too (R15): today's mix, measured on how its holdings moved in the past. #}
Ex-ante
Measured on today's mix as if you had held it, at today's weights, through the whole window, rebalanced back to those weights every week. It answers "how has this mix behaved?" where ex-post answers "how did what I held behave?", so the two differ. It is still historical: how these holdings moved in the past, not how they will move. The window starts where every holding has prices, so a young holding shortens it; you can exclude that holding to measure the rest over longer.
Diversification ratio
The holdings' volatilities, weighted, divided by the mix's volatility. 1 means the holdings moved in lockstep, so mixing them smoothed nothing; higher means their ups and downs partly cancelled out. Needs two years of weekly returns.
Effective number of holdings
How many equal-sized holdings the weights amount to: 1 ÷ the sum of the squared weights. Ten holdings at 10% each give 10; one at 91% and nine at 1% give about 1.2. It looks at the money only, not at how the holdings move.
Share of risk
How much of the mix's historical volatility each holding accounts for, given its weight, its own volatility and how it moved with the rest. The shares add up to 100%. A holding with a larger share of risk than of the money moved a lot, or moved with everything else; a negative share means it lowered the mix's volatility, as a hedge does. The contribution is the same share in volatility points: they add up to the mix's volatility. Correlation with the mix says how closely each holding moved with the whole.
FX decomposition
For a holding priced in another currency, such as a US stock in dollars, how much of its risk to a euro investor came from the currency. It shows the volatility of the holding's weekly returns in its own currency, the volatility of that currency against the euro, the correlation between the two, and the volatility in euros, which combines all three. A negative correlation means the currency tended to rise when the holding fell, so it partly offset the holding's moves. It uses the same weeks as the share of risk, and the correlation needs two years of them.
Correlation matrix
How closely each pair of holdings' weekly euro returns rose and fell together, from −1 (always opposite) to 1 (always together), with the benchmark added for comparison. Each pair is measured over the weeks both have prices, so a young holding's squares cover less time than the others. In euros, two dollar assets share the dollar's moves, which raises their correlation: that is real risk for a euro investor.
Rolling window
The same figure measured again and again over a window that moves through history: here, the 1-year window ending at each month end, from the first full year of history. It shows how volatile, or how well rewarded, the holdings were at different times, where a single window gives one average. Each point is historical, not a forecast. A window with too few weekly returns has no point, and the line breaks there.
Rolling correlation
The correlation of two holdings, or of one holding and the whole mix, measured over the 52 weeks ending each Friday. Measured over a single year it is noisy, about ±0.14 either way, so a single week's move means little. The pattern is the point: correlations tend to rise in sell-offs, just when diversification is wanted most.
Market regime
A dated period in market history, such as a recession, a bear market, a central bank's rate cycle or a shock. For each regime your history overlaps, the risk page shows the historical return and max drawdown from the regime's start to its end, yours and the benchmark's side by side. A regime that began before your history, or hasn't ended, is measured on the part inside it. A short regime's drawdown is one path through it, not a typical fall.
Look-through
Your exposure counted through each fund to what it holds: 70% in a world fund that is 70% American companies is 49% of your mix in American companies. It uses each fund's latest published holdings, as of the date shown, so it is a snapshot, not a history. A fund with no holdings data can't be looked through; it is shown as "unmapped" rather than guessed. A company held directly and through a fund is one name.
Listing currency of holdings
The currency each company's shares trade in, counted through the funds. A world fund bought in euros is mostly dollar shares, so most of its currency exposure is the dollar, not the euro. It is a proxy: a company listed in dollars may earn its revenue anywhere. The trading currency beside it is what you pay in for what you hold.
Effective number (Herfindahl)
How many equal-sized exposures a table amounts to: 1 ÷ the Herfindahl index, the sum of the squared shares. Ten countries at 10% each give 10; one at 70% and the rest small give about 2. It counts only the known share: an unmapped fund or an unknown sector isn't counted as one large exposure, so the known share is shown beside it. Unlike the effective number of holdings, it looks through the funds.
